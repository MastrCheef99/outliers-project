The class of one's family matters because higher-class parents are more likely to raise their children in an environment that is conducive to learning and creating opportunities. (Per Chapter 4.3)
Access to technology allows for broader learning and more opportunities in technologically dominated jobs.
This simulation is too narrow in scope to represent nuanced arguments made by Gladwell on cultural background. However, Gladwell explains in Outliers an example of how pilots are affected by cultural power dynamics (Chapter 7), how Asian cultural environments are conducive to math learning (Chapter 8) and how Jewish cultural environments in the 20th century allowed for Jewish people's success in legal and clothesmaking jobs (Chapter 5) This number simply represents those concepts as how well your outlier's cultural environment is conducive to success.
Having a higher level of education generally produces more Outliers because of the skills learned and connections made.
Gladwell spent Chapters 3 and 4 of the book discussing IQ. According to Gladwell, a certain IQ threshold needs to be met for most people to be successful, but past a certain point, it doesn't really matter, and this is reflected in how the success score is calculated.
Sometimes, simple dumb luck determines certain opportunities that allow for success. For example, Chapter 2 of Outliers contains the story of Bill Joy. Bill Joy got lucky in several ways by choosing the right university (the University of Michigan), which happened to have a computer system that let him practice programming for as much time as he wanted. Joy didn't plan to be a programmer and chose the university because of that; he simply got lucky that it did.
